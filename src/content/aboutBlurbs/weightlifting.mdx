# Why Weightlifting?

"Why do you exercise" doesn't come up as often with weightlifting since its appeal tends to be pretty straightforward–but there's more to it than the obvious!\
I started weightlifting in college as a physical therapy thing, but only in 2017 did I finally get serious about it. Today it's an incredibly important hobby and lifestyle thing for me, and I really enjoy connecting with others over it.

## Physique/Vanity

![SelfieAmon](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/PaintoverFlex_Amon.png)_Gym-selfie paintover by [Amon](https://bsky.app/profile/amonsyd.bsky.social) (2022)_

Nowadays the #1 reason I lift weights is for my physique. The health benefits are in the back of my mind, and I love benefiting from the energy boost and resiliency I get, but very honestly I am mostly working to achieve an idealized self-image.

I'm a big fan of encouraging folks' vanity-related motivations. Societal stigma gives vanity a bad name, but I think caring about your physique for your \*own\* reasons is a fantastic reason to exercise.\
And honestly any motivation that gets you to exercise is a health win!

## Injury Mitigation

When I was a teenager, I began to have issues sleeping on my back–I'd wake up sore and in pain. Various trips to doctors and (unfortunately) a *chiropractor* revealed that I suffer from [degenerative disc disease](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/16912-degenerative-disk-disease).\
The general advice was just to stay healthy, exercise, and crucially: build muscle around the back to support the failing discs.

## Building or Choosing a Routine

This is the area that will stop most folks. It's very intimidating going or even thinking of going to a gym, just to see 100 machines and pieces of equipment and not knowing where at all to start.

And of course if you try and google this, you'll just find endless fitness influencers trying to sell you *their* program.\
Some of them do have pretty solid routines, but you really don't need to spend money to organize exercises that'll help you reach your goals.

> Small note, I'm assuming from here on that you have access to a full gym. Box gym, local, etc. Calisthenics and bodyweight exercises are tremendous, but they're not my wheelhouse

### Choosing a Split

First and simply, you need to decide how many days you want to lift weights a week, and then divide up your muscles into groups for that. This is called a "workout split". The idea of which is to maximize muscle growth *and* proper rest, depending on how often you lift.

<EmojiBlockquote emoji={eyy} size="sticker">
Recommendations TL;DR if you want to skip ahead:

- **2-3 days:** Full Body
- **4 Days:** Upper/Lower
- **5+ days:** up to you! Just don't do full body each day.
</EmojiBlockquote>

#### Full Body

![3-day full body diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/3dayfullbody.png '"Full Body" but not working your throat or hand muscles? Smh...')
_Always leave at least one day of rest in between a full-body workout!_

Generally the best suggestion for new lifters! Go to the gym 2 or 3 days a week, and try and hit each major muscle group at least once each time.

Prioritize the biggest muscles, and do them first if possible. Then work your way down.

Example routine:

| Exercise | # Sets |
|---|---|
| Flat Bench Press | 2-3 |
| Lat Pulls | 2-3 |
| Leg Press | 3 |
| Lateral Shoulder Raises | 2 |
| Bicep Curls | 2-3 |
| Tricep Pressdowns | 2-3 |
| Abdominal Cable Crunches | 2 |

The downside with full-body workouts is that they tend to be a bit longer, as you need more time to hit so many muscles.

#### Upper/Lower

![4-day Upper-Lower diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/4dayUpperLower.png)

This is my personal favorite split for three big reasons:

1. I genuinely feel that it strikes the perfect balance of hitting every muscle, and giving me the opportunity to hit them at least 2x a week with plenty of rest
2. Many lifters, myself included tend to overemphasize chest or back training compared to legs, and this forces a more appropriate split of effort
3. 4 Days of lifting is *plenty* of weightlifting. I personally think it's the sweet spot, allowing for plenty of rest and not making this hobby consume your life.

![Argent performing a bicep curl while cheekily looking back at the viewer](@assets/argent/ArgentCheekyFlex-ArtFight2024_Maxadisasta.png "Oh? You're challenging me? Come to out-curl this cobra?")_Art by: [maxadisasta](https://bsky.app/profile/did:plc:rpdr2mllqtkyl6wpgufwjqof)_

Currently (September 2026) I run this split with a 5th day on the weekend dedicated to just arms and shoulders. The 5th day is totally unnecessary for the average lifter but I add it because I love weightlifting and I prefer to replace the biceps & triceps exercises in my "Upper" days with more large muscle movements.

#### Push/Pull/Legs

![5-day Push/Pull/Legs diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/5daySplit.png)

Nearly the same as the Upper/Lower split, but you further split your Upper day into "Pushing" motions (chest, triceps, etc.) and "Pulling" (back, biceps). You can also further divide the leg day into a "leg" day and a "hinge" day where you do squats on one day, and deadlifts or RDLs on the other.

I ran this split many times in my early days, and the best thing I can say about it is it lets you try out a wide variety of exercises. It's certainly not a bad split, but I think splitting this far is kind of overkill, and overemphasizes variety compared to \*hitting each muscle at least 2x/week\*

#### Bro Split

![5-day bro split diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/broSplit.png) _It's like you're doing nothing at all..._

Evil. Bad. Nasty.

Look. Everyone has their favorite muscle or muscle group to train. For me it's chest. I'm an absolute chest goblin. Having an entire day to JUST do chest exercises? Insanely gratifying.

But don't do it. Resist the siren's call...this split will waste your time. Why? Because spending 5 days a week training every muscle exactly once is incredibly inefficient.\
Training a single muscle to death in a workout produces diminishing results as the fatigue breaks down form, and your muscle hits its limit for constructive breakdown. Rest is a vital component of muscle growth and development; working a high-priority muscle 2-3 days a week with 1-2 exercises each of those days will produce much more consistent growth.

Try the Bro Split if you want...it *is* kinda fun. But be good to yourself and don't stick to it.

### Which Exercises?

So you've picked a workout split. Good job! Now comes the fun part: choosing which exercises to do.

There are many ways to do this! If you can ask an experienced friend, go with that. Otherwise I recommend just going to your gym and looking around. Seriously! Just walk around, peek at machines, try them out. Or even just watch others doing exercises and decide if that looks fun to you.

If you're just starting out then your #1 goal the gym is to get yourself to enjoy being at the gym. Even just a little bit. Optimization can come later. Very few exercises at the gym are \*bad.\* At worst they're just not *efficient.* And that's okay.

#### The Big Four

The so-called "Big Four" of weightlifting are:

1. Bench Press
2. Deadlift
3. Squat
4. Military/Standing Press

These are all excellent exercises, and altogether train the vast majority of your largest muscles holistically. As **Compound** exercises, they may focus on one or two major muscles in the movement, but ultimately recruit many other muscles in your body to stabilize throughout the motion.

<Accordion>
<AccordionItem title="What is a Compound Movement?">
Think about a squat. Yes you're using your quads and hamstrings to lower yourself and raise yourself up again. But you're also using your glutes to keep your back in position, your hips and abdominal muscles to keep your core steady, and if you're holding weight on your back, you're tightening your lats to keep the weight stable.

Compound! 

Isolation exercises by contrast, typically use equipment or leverage to isolate one muscle or muscle group as best as possible. Something like a concentration curl:

![Argent Concentration Curls](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/ArgentCurls_NoMoreBiscuit.png)_Art by [NoMoreBiscuit](https://bsky.app/profile/morebiscuits.bsky.social)_

This exercise pins your elbow on your leg so that as you do your bicep curl, the rest of your body is stabilized so that you don't "cheat" and use your torso or shoulders to move the weight. Just biceps!

</AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

You do not need to do the Big Four to be successful in the gym. However, they're a great guide helping you decide what lifts \*to\* do!

Don't want to do a bench press? Find a machine that does the same motion–it'll likely be a fair replacement!

Not confident enough to do squats? Well, the leg press machine is just about a more stable squat anyways, so give that a shot instead!

#### The Machine Circuit

If you're a beginner, are intimidated by the options and want somewhere to start where the instructions are clearly laid out without having to talk to anyone, then may I introduce...

<InlineEmoji emoji={sparkles}/> The Machine Circuit <InlineEmoji emoji={sparkles}/>

![10 Matrix weightlifting machines](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/machineCircuit.png)_An example of a single-brand "circuit" of lifting machines_

[Gold's Gym](https://www.goldsgym.com/blog/gym-machine-workout-routine/) has a pretty solid guide on this, actually! But it's really pretty simple: nearly every medium or larger gym will inevitably have a selection of hyper-specific weightlifting machines, often of a single brand. Your workout routine is simply going through these machines, and performing the ones that fit your plan for that day!

Leg day? Find the leg press, the leg extension, and the hamstring curl machine. Oh? Spotted a calf raise? That's legs, try that too! "Leg Abduction...?" Heck, try that too!\
Most of these machines have the instructions printed right on them, so you won't even have to ask anyone for help.

### Volume vs. "Going Heavy"

This boils down to the question of how much weight are you lifting versus how many repetitions (reps) in a single set.

**Volume** approach: lift lighter, but perform more reps (\> 10, often 12-15 range).\
**Strength** approach: lift heavy, perform fewer reps (\< 10, ranges vary).

So how do you choose? To me, the choice is between strategically tiring out the muscle tissue (volume), or the entire Central Nervous System (CNS) pathway response (strength). Let me explain.

Volume has always been suggested to me as the \*bodybuilder\* path. It mechanically depletes the energy in your muscles and stimulates a simple but powerful "build more muscle" response.\
I like how this redditor explains it:

> Lower load/higher volume consumes more glucose, creates more constriction in the blood vessels, creates a lower oxygen environment. Higher threshold motor units begin to cycle in and out as they fatigue, generating a lot of lactate and related metabolites. This is a potent signaling trigger to make more muscle and increase local glucose storage in the muscle. A bigger muscle is a stronger muscle.
>
> [Reddit user millersixteenth](https://www.reddit.com/r/fitness30plus/comments/17kwzre/comment/k7arrrs/)

Strength meanwhile emphasizes overloading the entire lifting system. Yes, bigger muscle = bigger strength, but muscle alone doesn't equate to performance.\
Big muscle does translate to power output, but the CNS is the arbiter of technique and control. How do you squeeze every last drop of power out of a muscle? Training and practice! Weightlifting like any other sport rewards practice.

Training for strength means overloading your nervous system, your tendons, and your connective tissue. It makes them the "weak link" to a lift, rather than muscular fatigue. And thus, your body works to improve those weaknesses.

**So Which One?**

Here's my take: if you're new to lifting, go with volume. Training for volume will teach you the movements, and how they affect your body. High intensity/heavy weight training requires better technique to avoid injury, and benefits greatly from innately understanding your muscles, and how they move/feel.

I personally mix the two in my current workouts, but generally lean towards going heavy. But when I started out I nearly exclusively trained with volume.

## Tracking Progress

![Argent Concentration Curls](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/ArgentCurls_NoMoreBiscuit.png)_Art by [NoMoreBiscuit](https://bsky.app/profile/morebiscuits.bsky.social)_

How do you know you're performing a lift effectively? Not just form, but how do you know that when you finish a set it's meaningfully contributing to growth and progress?

Long-term, the answer is pretty simple:

1. Number go up?
2. How do you look in the mirror?
3. How are your clothes fitting?

But short-term...as in, in the moment, how do you KNOW you're doing a good job?

### Log It!

![An old picture from 2018 of my purple paper logbook with some exercises and dates written on it. There's a pen wedged between some pages. The notebook is sitting open on a weightlifting bench with 2 plates visible behind it](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/logBook.png)_Behold my ancient 2018 logs_

You absolutely must track your weights. I promise you will not meaningfully remember your weights from week to week well enough to ensure you're actually progressing.

You don't need a fancy app! I still have my old ratty log books from almost 10 years ago now. I regret not putting years on the dates...but those served me perfectly well.\
I recommend recording:

- Date
- Exercise name
- How much weight you lifted
- How many reps
- How many more reps could you have done if you had kept lifting

If you *do* want a fancy app, I really like Jeff Nippard's [Macrofactor Workouts](https://macrofactor.com/workouts/) app. It's my daily driver these days. And not to shill for it without the benefit of a commission but I really appreciate the slick multi-angle exercise videos.

## Diet and Nutrition

In my opinion, diet is so important that I think any serious "how to put on muscle and/or lose fat" guide should start with it.

simplistic manner as “good foods” and
“bad foods” is not only inconsistent

### Nutrition basics

If you want to change your body composition in any way–building muscle or cutting fat, the #1 thing to understand is **Energy In/Energy Out.** Gaining or losing body mass is just a matter of whether you're consuming more calories than you expend each day. Whether it's fat or muscle depends on your activity level.

If you haven't, check out this or any **Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE)** [Calculator](https://tdeecalculator.net/) to get a rough estimate of how many calories your body burns just to maintain your current lifestyle.

#### Macros
"Macros" refers to the 3 macronutrients: **Carbohydrates**, **Protein,** and **Fat**.\
Each Macro serves a multitude of important biological functions. A healthy diet will include some amount of each of these.\
Briefly:
- **Carbohydrates** = primary source of energy–the body has a very easy time using carbs for energy
- **Protein** = support for building muscle and repairing systems within the body
- **Fat** = protective cushion around organs and the body, also needed for hormone production

##### Macros and Calories
Along with their other functions, macronutrients also provide energy, measured in kilocalories.

> When you hear talk of "calories", people are usually talking about kilo-calories. Going forward, "calories" mentioned here actually refers to kilocalories.

To calculate how many calories are in your diet, you simply add up the macros in your diet, and multiply their amounts in grams by either 4 or 9 to find out how many calories they're contributing.
- 1g Carbohydrates/Protein = 4 calories
- 1g Fat = 9 calories
### Exercise vs. Diet
When it comes to researching "how do I build muscle or cut fat?", you'll usually find some statistic like "70-80% diet, 30-20% exercise", meaning simply that monitoring + changing your diet is much more important than tweaking your exercise routine.\
In my experience it's not usually welcome advice. It feels much more exciting and actionable to hear that if you just lift weights 3x a week you'll get the physique of your dreams. Changing your diet feels harder and less proactive than running a mile every day.

## Fitness YouTubers I like
It's risky to put trust in an influencer...but these guys haven't steered me wrong yet.

- [Jeff Nippard](https://www.youtube.com/@JeffNippard): The big-name "Science-based Weightlifting" YouTuber. I've been using his workout routines for years now. I also use his "Macrofactor" app for dieting, and the new Workouts one for tracking my workouts. High quality videos, and great science based approach.
- [Sean Nalewanyj](https://www.youtube.com/@Sean_Nalewanyj): Extremely knowledgeable, shorts-focused YouTuber. His style is pretty sardonic and sarcastic as he does a fair bit of non-dogpiling anti-bs takedown shorts to make a point about good practice.
- [Squat University](https://www.youtube.com/@SquatUniversity): The absolute GOAT of Physical Therapy. I bought his book, and I credit him with 95% of my success in the last 3 years in alleviating back pain. The TL;DR of his philosophy is that most pain can be fixed with building strength in stabilization muscles (rotator cuff, hip flexors, etc.). Highly recommend if you're in any chronic joint or muscle pain.
