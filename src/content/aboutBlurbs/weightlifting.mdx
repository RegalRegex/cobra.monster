# Why Weightlifting?

"Why do you exercise" doesn't come up as often with weightlifting since its appeal tends to be pretty straightforward–but there's more to it than the obvious!\
I started weightlifting in college as a physical therapy thing, but only in 2017 did I finally get serious about it. Today it's an incredibly important hobby and lifestyle thing for me, and I really enjoy connecting with others over it.

## Injury Mitigation

I really didn't get any advice, other than to keep coming back to the chiropractor for pain-control. Lol.

## Biology and Biomechanics!

This is just a primer to help paint a picture of what weightlifting does to the body, and how to use those mechanics to hit our physique and strength goals <InlineArgentEmoji emoji="smile"/>

### Mind-Muscle Connection

You're already using your muscles day-to-day doing all sorts of things. So aside from complicated lifting movements, where's the skill in weightlifting?

Getting stronger from lifting weights is a twofold process:

1. Building bigger, stronger muscles
2. Improving the Central Nervous System (CNS) connection pathways between your mind and your muscles.

Most of this guide is about how to achieve #1, but #2 is the key to unlocking faster and *safer* progress in the gym. 

It sounds like gym bro science but this is real.[^2] It's nearly as simple as "thinking about the muscle you're using as you use it." 

![Spanish Olympian Lydia Valentín performing the Clean and Jerk weightlifting movement as a sequence of still shots of the full movement](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/LydiaValentin_cleanandjerk.png)
_Spanish 2-time world champion Lydia Valentín performing the Clean and Jerk at the 2016 Olympics_

It's easiest to think about with the big impressive lifts, like deadlifts or squats. And it's another step more obvious when you consider movements like the Clean & Jerk (above).\
The idea is that you're not just \*doing the movement\*, you're visualizing the movement and specifically focusing on the correct muscles to tighten and activate at the right time.

The best way to understand this concept is to do a simple movement like a dumbbell bicep curl. Do it with a light weight, and just perform the movement normally. Then try again while specifically focusing on contracting your biceps. It'll sound goofy and feel a bit goofy, but this is what I'm talking about. It's a basic skill that compounds as you get into more complex and heavier lifts. It's important both for performance and safety.

### You Gotta Rest

I got bad news. If you want to build muscle in any meaningful magnitude...you're gonna have to fix your sleep schedule. Non-negotiable.

![Argent Whine](@assets/argent/stickers/babanasaur/whine.png)
_I know._

Turns out that from a biology standpoint when it comes to "what actually leads to building stronger muscles", \*lifting weights\* is a distant third to **Diet** and **Sleep**.

I promised myself I wouldn't go into the depths of muscle tissue biology in this guide, so pardon the simplification: building muscle means stressing and breaking down your existing muscle, and signaling to your body that it needs to grow back better and stronger.

<EmojiBlockquote emoji={frustrated} size="sticker">
I'll never use my biology degree for anything at this rate...
</EmojiBlockquote>

The long and short is that you should have **rest days** in your workout program (the number of which depends on your health, lifestyle, and interest!--but I'll say if you're lifting more than 5x days a week you're probably doing too much) and you should shoot for **8+ hours of sleep a night**.

Lifting weights is exciting, and once you get into it and start getting results it's pretty tempting to just go all out on your workouts. That's great! But take your resting seriously. 3 days/wk in the gym with healthy sleep is worth much more than 5 days/wk with bad sleep. Put that extra gym time into sleep instead and you'll come out on top!

>There is also a condition called *overtraining*. It's an [actual medical condition](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/overtraining) but it's not common, and you probably won't hit this point. But it's a good thing to keep in mind if you feel like you're not getting high-quality sleep, or can't shake off your workout fatigue.

## Building or Choosing a Routine

This is the area that will stop most folks. It's very intimidating going or even thinking of going to a gym, just to see 100 machines and pieces of equipment and not knowing where at all to start.

And of course if you try and google this, you'll just find endless fitness influencers trying to sell you *their* program.\
Some of them do have pretty solid routines, but you really don't need to spend money to organize exercises that'll help you reach your goals.

> Small note, I'm assuming from here on that you have access to a full gym. Box gym, local, etc. Calisthenics and bodyweight exercises are tremendous, but they're not my wheelhouse

### Choosing a Split

First and simply, you need to decide how many days you want to lift weights a week, and then divide up your muscles into groups for that. This is called a "workout split". The idea of which is to maximize muscle growth *and* proper rest, depending on how often you lift.

<EmojiBlockquote emoji={eyy} size="sticker">
Recommendations TL;DR if you want to skip ahead:

- **2-3 days:** Full Body
- **4 Days:** Upper/Lower
- **5+ days:** up to you! Just don't do full body each day.
</EmojiBlockquote>

#### Full Body

![3-day full body diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/3dayfullbody.png '"Full Body" but not working your throat or hand muscles? Smh...')
_Always leave at least one day of rest in between a full-body workout!_

Generally the best suggestion for new lifters! Go to the gym 2 or 3 days a week, and try and hit each major muscle group at least once each time.

Prioritize the biggest muscles, and do them first if possible. Then work your way down.

Example routine:

| Exercise | # Sets |
|---|---|
| Flat Bench Press | 2-3 |
| Lat Pulls | 2-3 |
| Leg Press | 3 |
| Lateral Shoulder Raises | 2 |
| Bicep Curls | 2-3 |
| Tricep Pressdowns | 2-3 |
| Abdominal Cable Crunches | 2 |

The downside with full-body workouts is that they tend to be a bit longer, as you need more time to hit so many muscles.

#### Upper/Lower

![4-day Upper-Lower diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/4dayUpperLower.png)

This is my personal favorite split for three big reasons:

1. I genuinely feel that it strikes the perfect balance of hitting every muscle, and giving me the opportunity to hit them at least 2x a week with plenty of rest
2. Many lifters, myself included tend to overemphasize chest or back training compared to legs, and this forces a more appropriate split of effort
3. 4 Days of lifting is *plenty* of weightlifting. I personally think it's the sweet spot, allowing for plenty of rest and not making this hobby consume your life.

![Argent performing a bicep curl while cheekily looking back at the viewer](@assets/argent/ArgentCheekyFlex-ArtFight2024_Maxadisasta.png "Oh? You're challenging me? Come to out-curl this cobra?")_Art by: [maxadisasta](https://bsky.app/profile/did:plc:rpdr2mllqtkyl6wpgufwjqof)_

Currently (September 2026) I run this split with a 5th day on the weekend dedicated to just arms and shoulders. The 5th day is totally unnecessary for the average lifter but I add it because I love weightlifting and I prefer to replace the biceps & triceps exercises in my "Upper" days with more large muscle movements.

#### Push/Pull/Legs

![5-day Push/Pull/Legs diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/5daySplit.png)

Nearly the same as the Upper/Lower split, but you further split your Upper day into "Pushing" motions (chest, triceps, etc.) and "Pulling" (back, biceps). You can also further divide the leg day into a "leg" day and a "hinge" day where you do squats on one day, and deadlifts or RDLs on the other.

I ran this split many times in my early days, and the best thing I can say about it is it lets you try out a wide variety of exercises. It's certainly not a bad split, but I think splitting this far is kind of overkill, and overemphasizes variety compared to \*hitting each muscle at least 2x/week\*

#### Bro Split

![5-day bro split diagram](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/trainingSplits/broSplit.png) _It's like you're doing nothing at all..._

Evil. Bad. Nasty.

Look. Everyone has their favorite muscle or muscle group to train. For me it's chest. I'm an absolute chest goblin. Having an entire day to JUST do chest exercises? Insanely gratifying.

But don't do it. Resist the siren's call...this split will waste your time. Why? Because spending 5 days a week training every muscle exactly once is incredibly inefficient.\
Training a single muscle to death in a workout produces diminishing results as the fatigue breaks down form, and your muscle hits its limit for constructive breakdown. Rest is a vital component of muscle growth and development; working a high-priority muscle 2-3 days a week with 1-2 exercises each of those days will produce much more consistent growth.

Try the Bro Split if you want...it *is* kinda fun. But be good to yourself and don't stick to it.

### Which Exercises?

So you've picked a workout split. Good job! Now comes the fun part: choosing which exercises to do.

There are many ways to do this! If you can ask an experienced friend, go with that. Otherwise I recommend just going to your gym and looking around. Seriously! Just walk around, peek at machines, try them out. Or even just watch others doing exercises and decide if that looks fun to you.

If you're just starting out then your #1 goal the gym is to get yourself to enjoy being at the gym. Even just a little bit. Optimization can come later. Very few exercises at the gym are \*bad.\* At worst they're just not *efficient.* And that's okay.

#### The Big Four

The so-called "Big Four" of weightlifting are:

1. Bench Press
2. Deadlift
3. Squat
4. Military/Standing Press

These are all excellent exercises, and altogether train the vast majority of your largest muscles holistically. As **Compound** exercises, they may focus on one or two major muscles in the movement, but ultimately recruit many other muscles in your body to stabilize throughout the motion.

<Accordion>
<AccordionItem title="What is a Compound Movement?">
Think about a squat. Yes you're using your quads and hamstrings to lower yourself and raise yourself up again. But you're also using your glutes to keep your back in position, your hips and abdominal muscles to keep your core steady, and if you're holding weight on your back, you're tightening your lats to keep the weight stable.

Compound! 

Isolation exercises by contrast, typically use equipment or leverage to isolate one muscle or muscle group as best as possible. Something like a concentration curl:

![Argent Concentration Curls](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/ArgentCurls_NoMoreBiscuit.png)_Art by [NoMoreBiscuit](https://bsky.app/profile/morebiscuits.bsky.social)_

This exercise pins your elbow on your leg so that as you do your bicep curl, the rest of your body is stabilized so that you don't "cheat" and use your torso or shoulders to move the weight. Just biceps!

</AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

You do not need to do the Big Four to be successful in the gym. However, they're a great guide helping you decide what lifts \*to\* do!

Don't want to do a bench press? Find a machine that does the same motion–it'll likely be a fair replacement!

Not confident enough to do squats? Well, the leg press machine is just about a more stable squat anyways, so give that a shot instead!

#### The Machine Circuit

If you're a beginner, are intimidated by the options and want somewhere to start where the instructions are clearly laid out without having to talk to anyone, then may I introduce...

<InlineEmoji emoji={sparkles}/> The Machine Circuit <InlineEmoji emoji={sparkles}/>

![10 Matrix weightlifting machines](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/machineCircuit.png)_An example of a single-brand "circuit" of lifting machines_

[Gold's Gym](https://www.goldsgym.com/blog/gym-machine-workout-routine/) has a pretty solid guide on this, actually! But it's really pretty simple: nearly every medium or larger gym will inevitably have a selection of hyper-specific weightlifting machines, often of a single brand. Your workout routine is simply going through these machines, and performing the ones that fit your plan for that day!

Leg day? Find the leg press, the leg extension, and the hamstring curl machine. Oh? Spotted a calf raise? That's legs, try that too! "Leg Abduction...?" Heck, try that too!\
Most of these machines have the instructions printed right on them, so you won't even have to ask anyone for help.

#### Machines vs. Cables vs. Free-Weights

![Meme comic of Argent using a chest fly machine. 4th panel says "I'm gonna be so good at huggin"](@assets/argent/SoGoodatHuggin_Sprout.png "Not gonna lie this comic lives rent-free in my head every time I use this machine. Giving better hugs is inspiring!!")
_Extremely accurate comic. Meme edit by [Sprout](https://bsky.app/profile/cytosprout.bsky.social)_

"Free-weights" refers to weightlifting movements that use non-machine weights like dumbbells, barbells, kettlebells...any kind of bell, really. lol.

Outside of doing an entire circuit of machines, there's nothing that limits them to just beginners in the gym. In my experience, having a mix of machine and free-weight exercises has worked best for me.\
Machines are very good at 2 things:

1. Isolating muscles and movements
2. Offer stability for complex movements

![A man performing leg extensions on a machine](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/legextension.png)
_A dastardly simple, yet difficult (and satisfying) machine to use_

The leg extension machine is a great example of these 2 qualities. 

**Isolation:** This machine focuses on working your quadriceps--a very large and complex muscle used heavily in squatting movements (along with many other muscles). Developing it in isolation is tricky, but a stable machine like this lets you focus on its one job: extending the lower leg.

**Stability:** There are other isolation exercises for the quads, like the cable leg extension. They're also effective but offer much less stability than a machine like this. Less stability = spending energy on other muscles, so it can mean a little less efficiency.

![A dual-cable machine stock photo](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/cableMachine.png)

Cable machines are difficult to describe because they're so dang versatile. They're still firmly in the "machine" category, but I separate it because of how unique they are. They enable us to perform movements in line with our muscle anatomy without sacrificing the effectiveness of the lift itself.\
If I had to pick a single piece of gym equipment out of everything out there, I'd 100% pick a machine like the one pictured above.

<EmojiBlockquote emoji={eyy} size="sticker">
Pulleys are magic!! Easily one of my top 3 of simple machines <InlineArgentEmoji emoji="triumph"/>
</EmojiBlockquote>

Ultimately there's upside or downside to free weights vs. machines. They will both grow your strength and muscle the same amount.[^3] Choose based on availability and preference. I highly recommend experimenting! You'll definitely find machines and lifts you like and don't like.

<EmojiBlockquote emoji={argentThink} size="emoji">
Lately I've been leaning more heavily towards free weights because my current fitness goals are holistic, and about improving overall core and hip stability.
</EmojiBlockquote>

### Volume vs. "Going Heavy"

![Screenshot of Workouts app listing exercises and rep ranges next to set numberings](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/setsReps.jpg "lately I nearly never go above 10 reps. If I do, that means I need more weight next time!")
_App or pen & paper, you can't have a workout routine without deciding how many times you're actually doing a lift!_

This boils down to the question of how much weight are you lifting versus how many repetitions (reps) in a single set.

**Volume** approach: lift lighter, but perform more reps (\> 10, often 12-15 range).\
**Strength** approach: lift heavy, perform fewer reps (\< 10, ranges vary).

So how do you choose? To me, the choice is between strategically tiring out the muscle tissue (volume), or the entire Central Nervous System (CNS) pathway response (strength). Let me explain.

Volume has always been suggested to me as the \*bodybuilder\* path. It mechanically depletes the energy in your muscles and stimulates a simple but powerful "build more muscle" response.

I like how this redditor explains it:

> Lower load/higher volume consumes more glucose, creates more constriction in the blood vessels, creates a lower oxygen environment. Higher threshold motor units begin to cycle in and out as they fatigue, generating a lot of lactate and related metabolites. This is a potent signaling trigger to make more muscle and increase local glucose storage in the muscle. A bigger muscle is a stronger muscle.
>
> [Reddit user millersixteenth](https://www.reddit.com/r/fitness30plus/comments/17kwzre/comment/k7arrrs/)

Strength meanwhile emphasizes overloading the entire lifting system. Yes, bigger muscle = bigger strength, but muscle alone doesn't equate to performance.\
Big muscle does translate to power output, but the CNS is the arbiter of technique and control. How do you squeeze every last drop of power out of a muscle? Training and practice! Weightlifting like any other sport rewards practice.

Training for strength means overloading your nervous system, your tendons, and your connective tissue. It makes them the "weak link" to a lift, rather than muscular fatigue. And thus, your body works to improve those weaknesses.

**So Which One?**

Here's my take: if you're new to lifting, go with volume. Training for volume will teach you the movements, and how they affect your body. High intensity/heavy weight training requires better technique to avoid injury, and benefits greatly from innately understanding your muscles, and how they move/feel.

I personally mix the two in my current workouts, but generally lean towards going heavy. But when I started out I nearly exclusively trained with volume. 

## Tracking Progress

![Argent Concentration Curls](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/ArgentCurls_NoMoreBiscuit.png)_Art by [NoMoreBiscuit](https://bsky.app/profile/morebiscuits.bsky.social)_

How do you know you're performing a lift effectively? Not just form, but how do you know that when you finish a set it's meaningfully contributing to growth and progress?

Long-term, the answer is pretty simple:

1. Number go up?
2. How do you look in the mirror?
3. How are your clothes fitting?

But short-term...as in, in the moment, how do you KNOW you're doing a good job?

### Log It!

![An old picture from 2018 of my purple paper logbook with some exercises and dates written on it. There's a pen wedged between some pages. The notebook is sitting open on a weightlifting bench with 2 plates visible behind it](@assets/aboutPages/weightlifting/logBook.png)_Behold my ancient 2018 logs_

You absolutely must track your weights. I promise you will not meaningfully remember your weights from week to week well enough to ensure you're actually progressing.

You don't need a fancy app! I still have my old ratty log books from almost 10 years ago now. I regret not putting years on the dates...but those served me perfectly well.\
I recommend recording:

- Date
- Exercise name
- How much weight you lifted
- How many reps
- How many more reps could you have done if you had kept lifting

If you *do* want a fancy app, I really like Jeff Nippard's [Macrofactor Workouts](https://macrofactor.com/workouts/) app. It's my daily driver these days. And not to shill for it without the benefit of a commission but I really appreciate the slick multi-angle exercise videos.

## The Tough Stuff

![A sketch-style drawing of Argent next to a smaller anthro moth character. Briefly I want to address the roadblocks to weightlifting. Things that I and many others go through as we progress from beginners to advanced in this sport.

The physical barriers are mostly obvious, but coupled with the mental hurdles and the fact that weightlifting can be an innately vulnerable experience in public, I want to share some of my learned experience in the field.

### The Comparison Pitfall

![Argent as a shark kind of arm wrestling with a massive yellow and white shark monster](@assets/argent/SharkifiedArgent+Sunny_Cinna.png)_Art by [Cinna](https://bsky.app/profile/cinnaroni.bsky.social)_

So you're at the gym for the first time, and immediately everything is overwhelming and intimidating....

First and foremost it will be impossible not to compare yourself to others, so don't feel bad if you catch yourself doing it. I wish I could say "let someone else's physique motivate you!" But that's just the bodybuilding equivalent of telling a depressed person to "cheer up", so no that's not an option. 

It's also intimidating being new at a gym, and feeling out of place. It'll feel like people are always looking at you, and that you're just one misstep from a grievous faux pass. It's not true, of course, but let's talk about these feelings.

#### Do I Not Belong Here?

![Chibi Argent chest-bumping another black snake naga](@assets/argent/ToySnakes_Gifted_Falconaris(birthday2024).png)

It's impossible to feel like a regular on your first day. But keep showing up and making your best effort, and you'll feel right at home. I promise. And if you're really feeling nervous about it I cannot recommend enough the power of *bringing a friend.* Especially potent if they're already a regular there!

I would have never gotten this far without my friend James I met in Seattle who showed me the best ways to put a routine together. :3

And if you're worried that people are looking at you, here are a some reasons why someone at the gym might be looking at you:

1. They're waiting for your machine/equipment to become free and trying not to look like they're waiting so that you don't feel rushed
3. They're curious about what kind of exercise you're doing

### Motivation

![ARgent cheering on his friend, a brown werewolf with curly hair performing a preacher curl with a 30lb dumbbell](@assets/argent/DafCurlsMotivation.png)_Old art but ALWAYS a vibe!_

How the heck do you get and STAY motivated? Initial motivation can be kind of easy to find--maybe it's New Years, maybe a friend finally convinced you to try out the gym, or maybe you got hurt recently and recovery has encouraged you to seek strength.\
It can really be anything. But the first motivation is often not a lasting motivation.

So, what keeps you coming back? Ultimately it has to be something for \*you.\* Your goal in weightlifting is to hit a point where you're self-motivated, where you've made enough progress or gained enough confidence that you simply look forward to the experience.

Here are my tips for chasing that in brief:

1. Make it a social obligation--i.e. schedule weekly workouts with a friend
2. Make it routine, and give yourself something to look forward to after or during every workout (a podcast, a TV show, a small treat)
3. Pick a hyper-specific goal, and chase that--maybe you want 13in. biceps--be indulgent and make your early routines heavily focus that until you make progress!
4. Fall in love with your gym space. This could mean making friends there, or just really enjoying a movement, exercise, or machine that just feels *good*
5. Let others follow your gym journey--either social media or just talking to friends about it. Let people celebrate you!

And finally, check in with yourself as you go along. Explicitly ask your close friends for validation or support! It's okay to literally ask for them to clap for you for going to the gym twice this week.

This is HARD. You are literally giving up your valuable free time to go lift rocks and set them back down. All because science + people have told you it can give you results. And true as it is, it's undeniable that we humans struggle to believe something we can't see or experience ourselves.

Game the system--use whatever it takes to get yourself past the beginning because I promise once you've hit your stride, once you've started to see or feel the results you've been chasing, it'll become addictive. ## Diet and Nutrition

If you want to change your body composition in any way–building muscle or cutting fat, the #1 thing to understand is **Energy In/Energy Out.** Gaining or losing body mass is just a matter of whether you're consuming more calories than you expend each day. Whether it's fat or muscle depends on your activity level.

If you haven't, check out this or any **Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE)** [Calculator](https://tdeecalculator.net/) to get a rough estimate of how many calories your body burns just to maintain your current lifestyle.

What's important to understand though is that any fad diet or fad "super food" etc. is hyper-simplified science at best. There's no such thing as bad foods, just bad diets.\
If it's very important for you to have a certain treat then the key is just finding a diet that works around it. Letting yourself have a tasty treat is important to a successful diet! The mental boost is just as important as the continued dietary rigor.

### Macros

"Macros" refers to the 3 macronutrients: **Carbohydrates**, **Protein,** and **Fat**.\
Each Macro serves a multitude of important biological functions. A healthy diet will include some amount of each of these.\
Briefly:
- **Carbohydrates** = primary source of energy–the body has a very easy time using carbs for energy
- **Protein** = support for building muscle and repairing systems within the body
- **Fat** = protective cushion around organs and the body, also needed for hormone production

Along with their other functions, macronutrients also provide energy, measured in kilocalories.

> When you hear talk of "calories", people are usually technically talking about kilo-calories. This probably won't cause any issues, but keep it in mind with calculations.

To calculate how many calories are in your diet, you simply add up the macros in your diet, and multiply their amounts in grams by either 4 or 9 to find out how many calories they're contributing.
- 1g Carbohydrates/Protein = 4 calories
- 1g Fat = 9 calories

### Exercise vs. Diet

When it comes to researching "how do I build muscle or cut fat?", you'll usually find some statistic like "70-80% diet, 30-20% exercise", meaning simply that monitoring + changing your diet is much more important than tweaking your exercise routine. And unfortunately it's totally completely accurate.

It feels much more exciting and actionable to hear that if you just lift weights 3x a week you'll get the physique of your dreams. And in the early days that will seem like it's working. But you'll hit a wall before long, because bigger muscles need more energy, and if you don't compensate your growth with your diet, then your progress will stagnate.

The same goes in reverse for cutting fat--as you lose weight you'll need to continue to update your diet to maintain a proper *calorie deficit* to keep losing fat.

## Rapid-Fire FAQ:

    1. **What about Cardio?**
    - Do it if you can, and if you enjoy it--just try and space it out from your weightlifting (i.e. rest)
    2. **How can I avoid getting injured?**
    - Rest well, and focus on technique. Master mind-muscle connection before pushing super heavy. If you're already injured or at higher risk, consult a doctor or physical therapist and find exercises for your situation
    3. **What kind of gear do I need?**
    - Nothing special. Workout clothes you enjoy, and ideally shoes with flat, stable soles and wide toeboxes
    4. **Should I stretch before lifting?**
    - Up to you, just don't overdo it. In my opinion, it's more important to do warmup exercises before big lifts. Example: [Cable or Dumbbell Rotator Cuff warm up](https://xcelerategyms.com/rotator-cuff-warm-up/) before performing bench press

## Glossary of Terms
